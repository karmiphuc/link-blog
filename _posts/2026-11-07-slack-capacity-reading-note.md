## What is worth keeping

The article separates keeping resources busy from achieving useful results. A person with spare capacity can respond to an unexpected request immediately; filling that capacity creates queues and delays elsewhere. Slack can be time, money, staffing or room to change expectations.

Spare capacity also lets people reconsider direction and experiment. A calendar packed with operational work leaves no room to improve the operation itself. The article cautions that excessive slack can waste resources, while short constraints sometimes help creativity. Its argument is against treating maximum utilization as the only goal, not against useful effort.

## A weekly experiment

This is an application of the argument, not a schedule prescribed by the source:

1. Look at last week's interruptions. Which were predictable in kind, even if their timing was unknown?
2. Leave an explicitly unallocated block for that class of work. Start small enough to protect it in practice.
3. When it gets consumed, name what displaced it. Do not silently count the same hour against two commitments.
4. At the end of the week, compare waiting time and unfinished work with the previous week.
5. Adjust the reserve using what happened. An empty block is not automatically a failure; it may be the option to respond that mattered.

If the reserve is exhausted every week, the response is a capacity or priority decision, not another promise to fit everything in. No universal percentage of free time is established by this article.
